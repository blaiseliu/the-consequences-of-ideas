## The Monologium

Now, when he begins to develop his arguments for the existence of God, before we get to his most famous one, he started with, with a simple argument in the, uh, in the monologue, and that is this: that if anything exists — or just put it another way — whatever exists either exists through something, or by means of something, or it exists through nothing. That's a very simple, uh, distinction: either something, whatever exists in this world, exists because it has been made by something other than itself, or it has come from nothing, and the point that Anselm wants to establish at the very beginning is that for something to come from nothing is a manifest logical absurdity.

Now, keep in mind here that we've already looked at Augustine's doctrine of creation, where he says that God creates things acts nihilo, out of nothing, but what that means is that there wasn't some pre-existent matter that God then shaped and reshaped into the present world, something that eternally coexists with him, and what Augustine did not mean, when, when he said that creation is ex knee hello, that things come out of nothing by themselves — that is, with nothing causing them to be — because God is not nothing; God is something. And the whole reason why, uh, Augustine and Ansel and others are pointing to the necessity of a Creator is because of their conviction that it's manifestly impossible for something to come out of nothing by itself.

Now, I can't think of any principle more rational than that, and I can't think of any pit of irrationality that has been more frequently jumped into by modern thinkers than that — they use sometimes more sophisticated language, but, as I pointed out in my book, **Not a Chance**, that it still remains impossible for something to create itself, because, to create itself, it would have to be before it was, which means it would have to be and not be at the same time and in the same relationship. ## That Being Than Which No Greater Can Be Conceived

Now, the way Anselm states the argument is extremely important, because sometimes you can try to shorthand this argument and miss the whole point that Anselm was trying to communicate, and the way he did it was this: he says that God is that then which no greater can be conceived. Again, the shorthand version is often stated this way: that God is the greatest conceivable being. It's interesting that Anselm didn't state it that way; his, his articulation of it is much more awkward, but there was a reason for that: God is that being than which no greater can be conceived.

Now, that's, that's the, the primary premise that he adds to that: that for God to be that which then no greater can be conceived, he must exist in reality as well as in the mind — that is, he must have real being and not just hypothetical being — because if you're thinking simply of the idea of God that exists in your mind, you are not thinking about the God of Ansel, because God is saying — or Anselm is saying — that the God he's defining is the greatest being conceivable, and if you're conceiving of the greatest being conceivable as not existing, then we can come up with a greater being than the one you're talking about: the one who actually is, because existence is greater than non-being, or nothingness, in the language that he is using here.
